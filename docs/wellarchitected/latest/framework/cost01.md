

# Practice cloud financial management

 Cloud financial management is the practice of planning, tracking, and optimizing what an organization spends in the cloud. Cloud spend follows usage, so financial control depends on processes that expect variability. This capability covers the practices that make spend predictable and accountable: budgeting and forecasting for usage-based costs, setting measurable goals and targets, tracking resources through their project lifecycle, and staying current with new services and price reductions. 

 Cost is a shared responsibility across finance, engineering, and business teams. You need a shared vocabulary, a single source of cost metrics, and a regular review cadence. This capability describes how those teams plan and govern spend together.

## Capability intent
<a name="cost01-intent"></a>
+  Cloud spend is planned with forecasts that combine usage trends with the business events that drive demand, and budgets carry thresholds that alert owners before spend exceeds plan. 
+  Cost optimization has explicit goals and measurable, time-bound targets, tracked as efficiency such as cost per unit of business outcome rather than absolute spend alone. 
+  Finance, engineering, and business teams share a common cost vocabulary and a single source of cost metrics, and review progress on a regular cadence. 
+  Resources are tracked through their project lifecycle, so environments that no longer serve an active need are resized or retired rather than left running. 
+  The organization stays aware of new services, features, and price reductions, and revisits deferred decisions when the economics change. 

## Common issues to watch for
<a name="cost01-issues"></a>
+  Fixed annual IT budgeting is applied to elastic cloud spend, so the budget is wrong every month and overruns surface only when the invoice arrives. 
+  Forecasts are built from historical trends alone, which guarantees they miss every deliberate change the business makes, such as launches, campaigns, or regional expansion. 
+  Success is measured as absolute spend, so growth looks like failure and real efficiency gains stay invisible because no unit-cost metric separates the two. 
+  Goals are set without measurable, time-bound targets or a named owner, so cost optimization stays an intention that no review can assess. 
+  Resources outlive the projects that created them because no one owns retirement, and non-production environments run around the clock when they are used only in working hours. 
+  Monitoring new services and price reductions is nobody's explicit job, so architectures keep running on costlier options out of habit after cheaper ones ship.
