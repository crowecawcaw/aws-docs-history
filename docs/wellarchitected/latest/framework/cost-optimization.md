

# Cost optimization

 Cost optimization is a continual process of refinement and improvement across a workload's lifecycle. The practices in this paper help you build and operate cost-aware workloads that achieve business outcomes while minimizing costs, so your organization can maximize its return on investment. 

 A cost-optimized workload delivers business outcomes at the lowest appropriate price point while meeting its functional requirements and your performance, reliability, security, and sustainability standards. Cost optimization in the cloud differs from traditional on-premises environments because it emphasizes continuous improvement, automation, and alignment between resource consumption and business outcomes, rather than capacity prediction and procurement. 

 This pillar groups its guidance into four areas, each containing one or more capabilities that are made up of individual best practices. Cost optimization applies both when you first design a workload, when you set the baseline architecture, resource types, and monitoring, and as an ongoing operational practice, as you review decisions, adopt new services, and automate optimization over time. 

## Effective cloud financial management
<a name="effective-cloud-financial-management"></a>

 Cloud financial management treats spend as something you plan and steer, not a number you read after the fact. For a workload team, that means knowing what the workload should cost before it runs, setting targets you can measure against, and keeping those expectations current as services and prices change. This area covers building that practice at the workload level: budgets and forecasts, cost goals and targets, tracking spend across the project lifecycle, and staying current with new service releases that change the cost equation. 
+  [Practice cloud financial management](cost01.md) 

## Expenditure and usage awareness
<a name="expenditure-and-usage-awareness"></a>

 You cannot optimize what you cannot see. Awareness is knowing where spend goes, which team or product owns it, and whether the resources behind it are still needed. This area covers making spend visible and keeping it attributable: monitoring cost and usage against the teams and outcomes that create it, managing resources across their lifecycle so nothing runs longer than required, and planning for the data transfer costs that are easy to miss until they show up on the bill. 
+  [Monitor cost and usage](cost02.md) 
+  [Manage the lifecycle of resources](cost03.md) 
+  [Plan for data transfer](cost04.md) 

## Cost-efficient resources
<a name="cost-efficient-resources"></a>

 Once spend is visible, the next question is whether each part of the workload uses the right resource at the right price. A component can be cheaper because you picked a better-suited service, sized it to real demand, or matched its pricing model to how it actually runs. This area covers the decisions that set the unit price of a workload: evaluating cost when you select services, choosing the resource type, size, and number that fit the work, and selecting the pricing model that matches each component's usage pattern. 
+  [Evaluate cost when selecting services](cost05.md) 
+  [Select the correct resource type, size, and number](cost06.md) 
+  [Select the best pricing model](cost07.md) 

## Continuous optimization
<a name="continuous-optimization"></a>

 Cost efficiency is not a one-time project. Demand shifts, workloads change shape, and new services and pricing options appear, so a design that was efficient last quarter drifts without attention. This area covers keeping efficiency current: managing demand and supply so capacity tracks real need, reviewing and optimizing the workload on a cadence as requirements evolve, and automating the operational work so optimization runs with less manual effort. 
+  [Manage demand and supply resources](cost08.md) 
+  [Optimize over time](cost09.md) 
+  [Automate operations](cost10.md) 

 The goal across all four areas is not cost reduction alone, but the best ratio between cost and business value. For how to weigh cost against speed, performance, and other pillars, see [Understanding trade-offs](cost-understanding-tradeoffs.md). 

 This guidance is written for the technical, operations, business, and leadership roles responsible for building cost-aware workloads and managing cloud spend, including solutions architects, developers, DevOps and infrastructure engineers, site reliability engineers (SREs), product owners and project managers, FinOps practitioners, financial analysts and cloud financial controllers, engineering managers, IT directors, and executives such as chief technology officers (CTOs) and chief financial officers (CFOs). After reading it, you will understand the AWS recommendations and strategies to apply when you design and operate cloud architectures with cost in mind, so you can deliver business outcomes at the lowest appropriate cost while keeping the agility and innovation the cloud enables.
